How to Negotiate With Wedding Vendors (Scripts Included)

By Olivia Bennett June 07, 2026 6 min read

How to Negotiate With Wedding Vendors (Scripts Included)

That number on the contract? It is not the price. It is the first offer.

I learned this the hard way. Early in my planning career, I booked a florist for a client's wedding. She quoted $4,200. The client said "that seems high." I told her "that is the price." I did not negotiate. I did not ask for a breakdown. I just accepted. I left $1,200 on the table that I could have saved.

I never made that mistake again.

Vendors expect you to negotiate. Not all of them — the high-end, booked-out ones might not budge. But most small vendors, independent photographers, local florists, even venues — they build in margin. They expect you to ask. And if you do not, they keep it.

Let me give you actual scripts. Not "be polite and ask for a discount." Actual words you can say on the phone or type in an email.

For the venue, try this: "We love your space. Our total budget for venue and catering is $X. Can you work with us to get close to that number?"

That is it. You are not asking for a handout. You are telling them your constraint and asking if they can meet it. Some can. Some will offer a different date, shorter hours, or a stripped-down package. One couple I worked with got a $4,000 discount just by asking that exact question. The venue offered them a Friday in March instead of a Saturday in June. They took it.

For photographers: "Your portfolio is beautiful. We would love to work with you. Our photography budget is $X. Is there a way to customize a package to fit that?"

Many photographers offer "a la carte" options they do not advertise. Fewer hours, no second shooter, digital files only (no album). A client of mine wanted a $5,000 photographer but had a $3,000 budget. She asked. The photographer offered a 4-hour "elopement-style" package for $3,200. She booked it. She did not need 8 hours. She just needed the ceremony and portraits.

With florists: "Could you give me a quote for using more greenery and fewer expensive blooms? I love your style but need to keep costs down."

Florists want to work with you. They would rather adjust than lose the booking. Greenery is cheaper than roses. Local seasonal flowers are cheaper than imported. You can get the same aesthetic for half the price by swapping out the expensive flowers. Ask.

For catering: "We are considering a buffet instead of plated. Can you give us a quote for both options? Also, can we supply our own alcohol?"

Buffets are 20-30% cheaper than plated dinners. Supplying your own alcohol (buying from Costco or a local liquor store) can cut your bar bill in half. Caterers often charge $20-30 per person for alcohol. You can buy a bottle of wine for $10 that serves 4-5 people. Do the math.

You will see that switching from plated to buffet saves $12-15 per person. For 100 guests, that is $1,200-1,500. That is real money.

Now, let me tell you about the best negotiation I ever witnessed. Anna wanted a specific venue. The price was $10,000 for a Saturday in August. She asked for a Friday in September. The venue came back at $6,500. She asked what else could be included. The venue threw in tables, chairs, and a sound system — another $1,000 value. She signed. She saved $4,500 and got more than the original package.

She had a line item for "venue and rentals" of $9,000. The original quote was $10,000 plus $1,000 for rentals. She needed to get to $9,000 total. The negotiation got her to $6,500 for the venue plus free rentals — total $6,500. She saved $2,500 from her original budget and moved that money to her honeymoon.

One more script. This one is for after you have already gotten a quote.

For any vendor, after you get the quote: "Thank you for the quote. We really want to work with you. To be transparent, we have another vendor offering a lower price. Is there any flexibility in your quote?"

This works best when you actually have another quote. But even if you do not, you can say "we are trying to stay within our budget." Vendors would rather lower their price than lose the booking. I have seen photographers drop by $500, DJs drop by $300, florists add free delivery, venues waive the service charge. All from one sentence.

Now, a word about what not to do. Do not ask for a discount because "it is a small wedding" or "we are on a tight budget." Those are not reasons. Vendors hear them all day. Instead, ask for value. "Can you include X?" or "Can we adjust the package to fit our needs?" The word "discount" signals you want something for nothing. The phrase "customize a package" signals you are serious and flexible.

If a vendor will not budge, run the numbers on a DIY alternative. You might find that a backyard wedding with rentals is still cheaper than a venue that will not negotiate. Or you might find that the venue is worth it. Either way, you have data, not emotions.

Sam and Jordan wanted a venue that refused to negotiate. The price was $14,000 for a Saturday. No flexibility. Sam was ready to sign anyway. Jordan ran the backyard analyzer. A tent, tables, chairs, portable bathrooms, generator, and a coordinator would cost $8,000. They chose the backyard. They used the $6,000 savings to hire a live band. The band was amazing. They still talk about it. The venue? They forgot its name.

Negotiating is not about being aggressive. It is about being prepared. Know your budget. Know your alternatives. Be polite. Be honest. And ask.

A no is the worst they can say. And a no costs you nothing.

Anna — the one who switched to September — she saved $4,500 and used the money to add a videographer. She told me "I barely remember the venue. I watch the video every anniversary."
