I Planned a $15k Wedding in Portland That Looked Like $50k

By Olivia Bennett June 01, 2026 9 min read

I Planned a $15k Wedding in Portland That Looked Like $50k

Three years ago, I stood at the base of Multnomah Falls with a $14,800 spreadsheet on my phone and a dress I bought off the rack at Nordstrom Rack for $220. My now-husband Daniel was adjusting his tie — a $45 tie from a vintage store on Hawthorne. Our photographer? A former student of his who needed a portfolio. She charged us $600. The flowers? I grew some of them. The rest I bought from a local farm the morning before, stems still wet from the sprinklers.

People still ask me how we pulled off a wedding that looked like it cost $50,000. My answer is usually the same: we said no to almost everything the industry told us to say yes to.

Eight years as a wedding planner taught me something most couples never hear. I have seen people spend $80k on a single day and regret it. I have seen people spend $5k on a backyard ceremony and cry because they were not stressed. The difference is not how much you spend. It is where you spend it.

As a planner, the biggest lie I sold was "You get what you pay for." Sometimes you do. Sometimes you do not. The $8,000 photographer I booked for a client once showed up late, delivered mediocre edits, and spent more time posting behind-the-scenes content to her Instagram story than actually shooting. The $1,200 photographer I hired for my own wedding showed up early, stayed late, and sent me 400 edited photos within two weeks. She was building her portfolio. She cared more. She had something to prove.

So here is the raw breakdown of where $14,800 went in Portland, Oregon, 2023 — and how it ended up looking twice that.

Venue: $3,200. We rented a private estate in the West Hills through a site that allows hourly rental for events. Not a "wedding venue." Not a barn with a mandatory catering contract. Just a beautiful house with gardens, owned by a retired couple who rented it out for photo shoots and small gatherings. They charged $300 an hour. We booked 8 hours plus two hours of setup the day before. Total $3,200. The same property, if branded as a wedding venue, would have been $8,000-12,000 with a required vendor list.

Catering: $3,800. We did not hire a wedding caterer. We hired two local food carts — a wood-fired pizza truck and a taco cart — and let guests order what they wanted. Minimum spend was $2,500 plus gratuity. We ended up at $3,800 including two kegs of local beer and a batch of signature cocktails made by a friend who bartends. No sit-down dinner. No 22% "service charge" that does not go to staff. Just good food, served hot, with paper boats and compostable forks. People loved it.

Photography: $600. Portfolio-building student. I would do this again in a heartbeat.

Flowers: $340. This took work. I am not going to pretend it was effortless. Three weeks before the wedding, I visited a local cut-flower farm — there is one in Sauvie Island that sells to the public every Saturday. I bought buckets of dahlias, zinnias, and eucalyptus for $180. I stored them in buckets of water in my garage. The morning of, my mother-in-law and I trimmed and arranged them in mason jars we had collected from thrift stores ($0.50 each). The other $160 went to potted ferns from Home Depot that doubled as centerpieces and then became houseplants. No floral designer. No foam. No waste.

Attire: $450. My dress $220. Alterations $80. Daniel's vintage tie $45, shirt $50 from Uniqlo, pants he already owned. Shoes were our regular shoes.

Music: $0. Spotify premium playlist on a borrowed Bluetooth speaker. No DJ. No band.

Invitations: $120. Digital save-the-dates (free), printable invitation template from Etsy ($15), printed at FedEx Office ($40), envelopes from Amazon ($15). We skipped RSVP cards — guests replied via a free wedding website.

Hair and makeup: $200. I did my own makeup after watching YouTube tutorials for a month. Hair was a friend who does braids as a side hustle.

Marriage license and officiant: $250. License fee $60. Daniel's uncle got ordained online for free, we paid him $190 as a thank-you.

Miscellaneous: $1,840. This included chairs and tables rental ($800), portable dance floor ($300), string lights ($200 — we kept them), bathroom trailer rental because the house only had two bathrooms ($400), plates and cutlery from a party supply rental ($140). Not cheap, but still far less than a venue that includes these things at inflated "wedding package" prices.

Total: $14,800. Not a typo. No hidden line items. No "oh we forgot to budget for gratuities" surprises. We tracked every dollar in the Wedding Budget Calculator before we spent it.

That calculator saved us from ourselves. I would plug in a number, move sliders, see in real time how spending $1,000 more on flowers meant $1,000 less on food. It forced trade-offs. And trade-offs are the only way to stay sane.

Perfection was not the goal. The pizza truck ran out of one topping. A fern tipped over. Daniel's uncle spoke for twenty minutes during the ceremony, which felt like forty. But no one remembers those things. They remember dancing under string lights at midnight, the smell of eucalyptus, and the fact that we were not stressed.

Brides in $8,000 dresses crying in bathrooms because their $20,000 venue forgot the votive candles they paid an extra $900 for. Grooms fighting with their in-laws over a $500 upcharge for table linens. Those weddings did not look more beautiful. They just looked more anxious. I have watched this happen too many times.

Now, I am not saying everyone should do what we did. Some people want the ballroom, the sit-down dinner, the live band. That is fine — as long as you are not lying to yourself about what you can afford. The problem is not expensive weddings. The problem is borrowing money for a party, or draining your down payment fund, or going into credit card debt for Instagram photos.

My college roommate Jenna got married the same year we did. She and her husband spent $55,000 on a vineyard wedding. It was gorgeous. They also took out a $20,000 personal loan at 11% interest to cover the overage. Two years later, they are still paying $400 a month for a day that ended at midnight. She told me "I wish I had asked you for help."

Watching couples fall in love with a vision and then get crushed by the bill — that is why I left planning and started writing. Because I got tired of seeing it.

I ran our wedding through this analyzer after the fact. A traditional venue with similar capacity would have cost us $10,000 more for the space alone. When you add in mandatory catering minimums ($8k vs. our $3.8k), forced vendor lists ($2k+), and "administration fees" (often 5-10% of everything), our total would have been $32k. More than double.

Nobody in the industry says this out loud. Wedding vendors mark up everything because they can. The same photographer who charges $8,000 for a wedding will shoot a corporate event for $2,500. The same venue that charges $12,000 for a Saturday wedding will rent for $4,000 on a Tuesday for a nonprofit gala. The word "wedding" adds zeroes. I used to hate this. Now I just help people work around it.

If you are planning a wedding right now, here is the best advice I can give you, and it does not cost anything.

Look at your spreadsheet — or start one. See where the big numbers are. Usually it is venue, catering, photography, and flowers. For each of those, ask yourself: "Is this something I truly care about, or something I have been told to care about?"

We cared about good food and a pretty outdoor space. We did not care about a fancy dress, professional florals, or a DJ. So we spent money on pizza and a garden, and saved everywhere else. That is the whole trick.

I ran flowers through this tool. DIY cost $340 plus about 8 hours of my time. Hiring a florist would have been $2,000 minimum. That is $1,660 saved, or $207 per hour of my time. Worth it. I ran wedding cake — a friend offered to make a simple two-tier. DIY cost $40 in ingredients. Bakery quote was $350. Savings $310, time 3 hours = $103 per hour. Still worth it. I ran invitations — digital save-the-dates + printed invites. DIY cost $120. Designing and ordering custom invites would have been $600. Savings $480, time 5 hours = $96 an hour. Also worth it. But I also ran "sewing my own dress" — the tool said estimated materials $150, time 40 hours, savings $70. That is $1.75 an hour. Not worth it. I bought the dress.

That tool stopped me from making stupid decisions.

A lot of people ask me: "Would you change anything about your wedding?" I think about this sometimes. The pizza truck ran out of the mushroom truffle pizza by 7 PM. I was sad for about seven minutes. Then I ate a taco. I also wish I had assigned someone to refresh the flower water — the dahlias got droopy by 9 PM. But honestly? No. The imperfections are what make it ours.

One last thought before I finish. Your wedding is one day. Your marriage is supposed to be the rest of your life. Starting it in debt because you wanted a champagne wall or a twelve-piece band is not the move. I have never met an older couple who wished they had spent more on their wedding. I have met many who wished they had spent less.

Jenna and her husband finally paid off that $20k wedding loan last month. They celebrated by going out to dinner at a food cart pod. She texted me: "This meal cost $34. I am happier than I was at my $55k wedding." I believe her.

Olivia Bennett, Portland
