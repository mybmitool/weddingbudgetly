Elopement Vs Wedding Cost Comparison 2026

2026-07-23 · 2 min read · General
General

My friend spent $38,000 on her wedding last year. One hundred twenty guests. Open bar. Live band. Five-tier cake. It was beautiful. It was also a financial anchor that she is still dragging. She and her husband delayed buying a house for two years because of the wedding debt. They are paying $400 a month on a personal loan they took out for the reception. For a party that lasted six hours.

My partner and I eloped. Courthouse ceremony. Two witnesses. Dinner at our favorite restaurant after. Total cost: $3,200. The rings were $1,800. The marriage license was $75. The dinner was $180. The photographer — a friend with a nice camera — was $400. My dress was $450. His suit was $295. And that was it. We were married. We were happy. And we were not in debt.

The wedding industry hates elopements. They call them "less special." They say you will regret not having the big day. They sell the fantasy of the perfect wedding — the dress, the venue, the flowers, the photos — and they charge 300% markup for all of it. A $2,000 dress costs $400 to make. A $15,000 venue costs $3,000 a night any other weekend. The wedding tax is real, and it is predatory.

But here is what the industry will not tell you: the marriage matters more than the wedding. My friend with the $38,000 wedding? Divorced in 18 months. The stress of the debt, the pressure to please everyone, the exhaustion of planning — it broke them. I am not saying expensive weddings cause divorce. But I am saying that starting your marriage with $25,000 in debt (after gifts) is not a strong foundation.

Micro-weddings are the middle ground. Twenty guests. A nice venue. Good food. No debt. My cousin did this. She spent $8,000. Close family only. A brunch instead of a dinner. A Spotify playlist instead of a band. A Costco cake instead of a bakery. It was intimate. It was joyful. And she bought a house six months later because she had savings instead of debt.

The pressure to have a big wedding is social, not financial. Your parents want it. Your friends expect it. Instagram shows you everyone else's perfect day. But Instagram is not real. Those perfect weddings have credit card debt behind them. They have family arguments. They have stress that does not show in the photos. What shows is the highlight reel. The behind-the-scenes is messier.

If you are getting married, ask yourself: what do we actually want? If the answer is a big party with everyone you know, budget for it. Save for it. Do not borrow for it. If the answer is just to be married, elope. Have a micro-wedding. Spend the money on a house, a trip, a future. Because the wedding is one day. The marriage is decades. And decades deserve better than a debt hangover.

— Jennifer Walsh, Dallas, TX

Jennifer is a wedding budget expert who eloped and never regretted it. She believes in marriage, not mortgage-level weddings.
