Wedding Catering on a Budget: Buffet vs. Plated vs. Food Truck

By Olivia Bennett June 13, 2026 7 min read

Wedding Catering on a Budget: Buffet vs. Plated vs. Food Truck

Alex held three catering proposals in his hands. His fiancé Sam stood next to him, arms crossed, reading over his shoulder. One proposal said "plated dinner — $85 per person." Another said "buffet — $65 per person." The third said "food truck — $30 per person plus minimum." They had 100 guests. Alex did the math out loud. $8,500 vs $6,500 vs $3,000.

"That is a $5,500 difference," he said. "For the same people."

Sam uncrossed her arms. "Food truck," she said. "Done."

Not every couple decides that fast. Most default to plated dinners because "it is more elegant." Then they spend the next two years paying off the credit card bill. Some choose buffets because "it is cheaper." Then they run out of food because the caterer underestimated the hungry uncle factor. Some food trucks kill it. Some food trucks run out of gas.

Let me decode the real costs, hidden fees, and guest experience for each option.

Plated dinner. Waitstaff brings each guest a pre-selected meal. Usually three choices — chicken, fish, vegetarian. Sounds fancy. Costs the most. Average $70-120 per person, not including tax, service charge, or alcohol. The hidden cost? Staffing. Plated dinners require more servers. More servers mean higher service charge. I have seen a $80 per person plate turn into $110 after fees.

Also, you have to collect meal choices from your guests. This is a logistical nightmare. People change their minds. People write "gluten-free" but do not mean it. People select the fish and then complain they wanted chicken. I have managed this for 200 weddings. It is always a mess.

Buffet. Guests serve themselves from shared stations. Average $40-70 per person. Fewer staff needed. Lower service charge. But you need to order 15-20% more food than a plated dinner because people take larger portions. I have seen buffets run out of the popular dish halfway through the line. Then you have angry guests and a scramble to order pizza.

Variety is the advantage. People can try a little of everything. Dietary restrictions are easier to manage. And buffets feel more social. Guests mingle in line.

Food truck. A truck or cart arrives and serves one type of food — tacos, pizza, burgers, BBQ. Average $15-30 per person, but with a minimum. Most food trucks have a $2,000-3,000 minimum, plus travel fees. For 100 guests, that is $20-30 per person. Much cheaper. But the line moves slower. You need 1-2 trucks for 100 people. One truck for 100 people means a 45-minute wait. That is too long.

Alex and Sam had 85 guests and a $12k total budget. They wanted to spend no more than $4k on food. Plated was out of the question. Buffet quotes came in at $5,500. Food truck quotes came in at $3,200. They chose two food trucks: a taco truck and a pizza truck. Total $3,800 with tax and tip. They had $200 left, so they added a popcorn machine for late night.

Guests loved it. People got tacos, then pizza, then tacos again. No one went hungry. No one complained about a long line because two trucks halved the wait. Alex and Sam stayed under budget.

They moved the $1,700 they saved from catering to their photography budget. That got them a second shooter for the whole day instead of just four hours. Their photos were amazing. The trade-off worked.

Here is the dirty secret of wedding catering. The per-person price is never the final price. Read the contract for these add-ons:

Service charge. Usually 18-22%. This is not gratuity. Ask where it goes. If it goes to the house, not the staff, tip separately.

Gratuity. 15-20% additional. On a $5,000 catering bill, that is $750-1,000.

Tax. 10-15% depending on your location.

Overtime. If your reception runs late, the staff charges $50-100 per hour per person. Four extra people for two extra hours = $400-800.

Cake cutting fee. Some venues charge $2-5 per slice to cut and serve your cake. That is $200-500 for a 100-person wedding. I have seen couples bring a cake and get a $300 surprise fee.

Corkage fee. If you bring your own alcohol, some venues charge $10-20 per bottle to open and serve it. That is $200-400 for a moderate bar.

Plate fee. If a vendor — like your photographer — needs a meal, that is another $40-70.

Dana got a catering quote for $6,500. After service charge, tax, and gratuity, her final bill was $9,800. She almost fainted. She had not budgeted for the fees. The contract was 14 pages. She did not read page 8. She learned to always ask for the "total out-the-door price" before signing.

Some venues require you to use their in-house catering. Others allow outside vendors. The analyzer shows you the difference. In-house catering often adds a 20-30% premium because they know you have no choice. If you can, choose a venue that allows outside catering. Competition lowers prices.

My honest ranking for budget-conscious couples:

Best for low budget (under $3k for 100 guests): DIY drop catering. Order trays of pasta, salad, and bread from a local restaurant. Hire two servers from a staffing agency. Use disposable plates and cutlery. Total cost can be under $2,500. I have done this for a backyard wedding. It worked.

Best for medium budget ($3k-6k for 100 guests): Food trucks or buffet from a mid-tier caterer. Food trucks are more fun. Buffet is more reliable. Pick based on your vibe.

Best for high budget ($6k-10k for 100 guests): Buffet with premium ingredients. Add a carving station or raw bar. Skip plated. You will save money and guests will still feel fancy.

Only choose plated if: You have 50 or fewer guests, or your venue requires it, or you have unlimited budget. Otherwise, it is not worth the premium.

Tom wanted plated because his mother insisted. His mother was not paying. I asked Tom: "Would you rather spend $8k on food or $6k on food and $2k on a videographer?" He thought about it. He chose the videographer. He told his mother "we are doing buffet." She got over it.

I do not recommend DIY for the main meal. Food safety is real. But desserts? Cookies, brownies, a small cutting cake — those are fine. I have seen couples save $500 by making their own dessert table.

One more tip. Skip the late-night snack. It is trendy. It is expensive. And most guests are too full from dinner to eat it. I have seen $500 worth of sliders go straight into the trash. Use that money for a better main meal.

Finally, ask your caterer about a "family-style" meal. It is like a buffet but served to tables in large bowls. Guests pass the food around. It is cheaper than plated, more elegant than buffet, and creates a warm, shared experience. I have done this for several weddings. Guests loved it.

Dana — the one who got the surprise $9,800 bill — she went back to the caterer and asked to remove the premium entree option. She switched from steak and salmon to chicken and vegetarian. Her final bill dropped to $7,200. Still more than she wanted, but $2,600 less. She told me "I should have asked for the total price upfront." Yes. Always ask.
