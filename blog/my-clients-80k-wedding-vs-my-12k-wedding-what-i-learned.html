My Client's $80k Wedding vs. My $12k Wedding: What I Learned

By Olivia Bennett June 04, 2026 7 min read

My Client's $80k Wedding vs. My $12k Wedding: What I Learned

Same planner. Two weddings. Sixty-eight thousand dollars apart. One couple still talks about their day like it was a dream. The other couple divorced before their first anniversary.

That is not drama. I say it because the difference was not the budget. The difference was what they were trying to prove.

In Portland, Emily and Jake met — both worked in tech, mid-twenties, salaries around $120k each. They had no debt, no kids, a comfortable savings account. They could afford a nice wedding. But $80k is not "nice." $80k is a down payment on a house. $80k is a year of college tuition. $80k is two new cars.

Early on, I asked Emily what she wanted. She said "I want it to be memorable. I want people to talk about it for years." I asked what kind of memories she wanted. She said "beautiful. Elegant. The kind of wedding you see in magazines."

We started planning. Venue: a renovated barn in the Willamette Valley that charged $12,000 for a Saturday in July. No tables, no chairs, no bathrooms. Those were extra. Catering: a high-end farm-to-table company, $18,000 for 110 guests. Flowers: a designer who specialized in "installation art." $9,000. Photographer: one of those $7,000 packages with engagement shoot, second shooter, and a leather-bound album. Band: $6,000 for eight pieces. Dress: $4,500 plus $800 alterations. Videographer: $4,000. Invitations: letterpress, $1,200. Favors: custom candles, $600. Rehearsal dinner: $3,500. Welcome bags for out-of-town guests: $800. Day-of coordinator (yes, she hired me even though I was already her planner — she wanted someone separate to run the day). That was another $2,500.

Total: $84,000. She cried happy tears when she saw the venue decorated. It was gorgeous. I am not going to pretend it was not.

But here is what happened behind the scenes.

For three months, Emily fought with her mother over the flower budget. She had panic attacks about the seating chart — who to put next to whom, who would be offended. She gained ten pounds from stress eating. The week of the wedding, she sent me a 2 AM text: "I do not even like this band anymore." The day of, her dress strap broke during the first dance. She laughed it off, but I saw her face in the dressing room after. She was gutted.

Guests had a wonderful time. The food was incredible. The band was loud. People danced. But Emily — I do not think she enjoyed her own wedding. She was too busy managing it.

Now, my wedding. $14,800 — let us just say $15k with the things I forgot to track. Not $12k, but close enough for the comparison. Friday date. Pizza truck. Spotify playlist. Dress off the rack. Flowers from a farm. No bridal party, so no drama. No rehearsal dinner — we took our parents to a brewery the night before and spent $400. No welcome bags. No favors. No videographer.

I did not cry during planning. I did not argue with my mother. I did not gain weight. I was not stressed. The day had minor disasters — the pizza truck ran out of one topping, the fern tipped over, my uncle talked too long. I remember laughing about the fern. I do not remember being upset.

I ran Emily's venue through this tool. The same property, but rented as a "private estate for an event" instead of a "wedding venue." Same Saturday, same hours. The tool estimated $7,000 instead of $12,000. Emily did not know that was an option. She went with the branded wedding venue because that is what she saw on Instagram.

That is not her fault. That is the industry.

Starting with a budget of $50k, Emily kept adding. By the time she added the barn, the farm-to-table, the installations, the band, she was at $70k. She did not move the sliders. She did not ask "what if I spend less on flowers and more on something else?" She just said yes to everything she wanted. The calculator would have forced her to choose. She did not want to choose. She wanted everything.

A year after the wedding, Emily called me. She and Jake were separating. She said "I spent $84,000 on a party for a marriage that did not even last a year." I did not know what to say. She said "I wish I had spent that money on us. On therapy. On a house. On anything but a single day."

That call broke something in me. I was still working as a planner then. After that, I started steering every client toward smaller, cheaper, more intentional weddings. Some listened. Some did not. The ones who listened thanked me later. The ones who did not — some are fine. Some are divorced. Some are in debt.

Expensive weddings do not cause divorce. That is not true. But couples who prioritize the performance over the partnership — who care more about what the photos look like than how they feel — those couples have a harder time.

My wedding was not perfect. The food truck ran out of food. I forgot to assign someone to refresh the flower water. Daniel's tie was too long. We did not have a first dance because neither of us wanted one. A guest got too drunk and fell asleep on a couch. I stepped in mud and ruined my shoes.

But here is the difference. On our first anniversary, Daniel and I looked at our wedding photos and laughed. We did not cringe. We did not fight. We ordered pizza from the same truck, sat on our couch, and said "that was a good day."

Emily and Jake did not make it to their first anniversary. Their wedding video is still on YouTube. Someone sent it to me once. Thirty seconds was all I could watch. It was beautiful. It was also a tombstone.

This is not meant to scare you. I tell you this because I want you to ask yourself one question before you sign any vendor contract: "If my marriage ended in five years, would I regret spending this?"

If the answer is yes, do not spend it.

Saving? Emily did not do that. She put it on credit cards, then used a bonus to pay them off. She never felt the pain of the money. It was abstract. If she had used the savings tracker — watched the number grow slowly, felt each deposit — she might have made different choices. Because when you save $200 a week for two years, every dollar has weight. You do not throw $9,000 at flowers. You ask "do I really want these flowers?"

That is not judgment. That is math.

My wedding savings took 18 months. I set up automatic transfers of $150 a week. I watched the balance go from $0 to $14,800. I knew exactly what I was spending because I had touched every dollar. That is why I could say no to the flower wall and yes to the pizza truck. I earned that money. I was not about to waste it.

If you are planning a wedding right now, I have one piece of advice. Do not spend money you have not saved. Do not go into debt for a party. Do not let your parents write a check with strings attached. And do not look at Emily and Jake and think "that will not be me."

Because that is what they thought too.

Last year, Emily remarried. She told me her second wedding cost $8,000. Backyard, potluck, Spotify playlist. She said "this one feels like us." I am happy for her. I just wish she had not needed to learn the lesson twice.
