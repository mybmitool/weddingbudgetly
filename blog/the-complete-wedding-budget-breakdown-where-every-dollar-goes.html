The Complete Wedding Budget Breakdown: Where Every Dollar Goes

By Olivia Bennett June 06, 2026 6 min read

The Complete Wedding Budget Breakdown: Where Every Dollar Goes

You are engaged. Congratulations. Now someone asks you "what is your budget?" and you freeze.

I have been there. I have also been the planner asking that question to 200 couples. Most of them have no idea what a realistic wedding costs. They throw out a number — $20k, $30k, $50k — based on what they think weddings cost. Then they start researching and discover that a photographer alone is $4,000. Panic sets in.

Let me save you from that panic. Here is exactly where your money goes in a typical wedding, based on real data from 200+ weddings I have planned or analyzed.

The average wedding in the US cost $36,000 in 2026. That is not a typo. That is the number. But that is an average. In Portland, Oregon, where I live, the average is about $32,000. In New York City, it is $55,000. In rural Iowa, it is $18,000. Location matters more than almost anything else.

Here is the breakdown of that $36,000 average:

Venue: 30% — about $10,800. This is the biggest line item. It includes the rental fee, tables, chairs, basic lighting, and often a "service charge" that adds 20%. If the venue requires in-house catering, the venue percentage is effectively higher because catering costs are bundled.

Catering: 25% — about $9,000. This includes food, drinks, servers, bartenders, and gratuity. Plated dinners cost more than buffets. Open bars cost more than beer and wine. Late-night snacks add $10-15 per person.

Photography and videography: 10% — about $3,600. Photos last forever. Video is increasingly popular. But you do not need a second shooter or a highlight reel if you are on a budget.

Flowers and decor: 8% — about $2,880. This includes bouquets, centerpieces, ceremony decor, and installations. This category is easy to overspend on because small additions add up fast.

Attire and beauty: 6% — about $2,160. Wedding dress, suit or tux, alterations, shoes, hair, makeup. This does not include engagement rings or wedding bands.

Music and entertainment: 5% — about $1,800. DJ, band, ceremony musicians. Live bands cost 2-3x more than DJs.

Rentals and logistics: 5% — about $1,800. Tents, dance floors, lighting, generators, portable bathrooms. This applies mostly to DIY venues like backyards or raw spaces.

Stationery: 2% — about $720. Invitations, save-the-dates, thank-you cards, postage. Digital invitations can cut this drastically.

Transportation: 2% — about $720. Getting the wedding party to the venue, guest shuttles, rental cars.

Gifts and favors: 2% — about $720. Bridesmaid/groomsman gifts, wedding favors for guests. Many couples skip favors entirely.

Miscellaneous: 5% — about $1,800. Marriage license, officiant fee, insurance, overtime charges, unexpected expenses.

These numbers are averages. Your wedding will look different. But this framework gives you a starting point.

You will see that adding 10 guests does not just add 10 meals. It adds 10 chairs, 10 plates, 10 glasses, 10 favors, and often a larger venue. The estimator shows you the true per-person cost. Once you see that each guest costs $150 on average, you will think twice about inviting your mother's neighbor's cousin.

Mike had a guest list of 180 people. His budget was $30k. The per-guest estimator showed $167 per person average in his city. That is $30,000 exactly — leaving zero room for anything else. He had to either increase his budget or cut guests. He cut to 140 people, freed up $6,700, and used that money to add a photo booth and a better DJ. He told me "cutting people felt mean at first. Then I realized I had not spoken to half of them in five years."

The most common mistake I see is under-budgeting for hidden costs. The breakdown above includes most of them, but let me call out the ones people forget.

Tax. In Portland, lodging tax on venues is 11.5%. On a $10,000 venue, that is $1,150. Many couples forget tax until they see the final contract.

Service charge. Many venues and caterers add a 20-22% "service charge" that is not gratuity. Read the contract. Sometimes it goes to staff, sometimes to the owner. Ask.

Overtime. Your DJ, photographer, and venue all charge extra for time beyond the contracted hours. One extra hour for a band can be $1,000. Plan your timeline carefully.

Delivery and setup fees. Florists charge $100-300 for delivery. Rentals charge for pickup. Photographers charge travel if your venue is outside their zone.

Gratuity. Vendors expect tips. Photographer $200-500, DJ $100-300, caterer 15-20% of food bill. This adds $2-3k to your total.

I added a 15% buffer line item called "unexpected." That buffer saved me when the bathroom trailer rental went up $200, when I needed extra string lights, and when my dress alterations cost more than quoted. Without that buffer, I would have been stressed. With it, I just pulled from the buffer and moved on.

Here is your action plan. Open the budget calculator. Enter your total number. Adjust sliders until they reflect your priorities. Then add 15% to every line item. That is your real budget. If that number scares you, start cutting — guests, upgrades, days of the week, anything. Do not borrow. Do not rely on gift money. Do not hope.

A realistic budget is the difference between a stressed engagement and a joyful one.

Jenna — the one who cut the late-night snack — she ended up under budget by $1,200. She used that money to upgrade her honeymoon hotel. She said "I do not even remember what the late-night snack was supposed to be. I remember the honeymoon."
