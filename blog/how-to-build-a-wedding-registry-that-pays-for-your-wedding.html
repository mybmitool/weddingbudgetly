How to Build a Wedding Registry That Pays for Your Wedding

By Olivia Bennett June 11, 2026 6 min read

How to Build a Wedding Registry That Pays for Your Wedding

Jenna walked into my office with a list. Not a guest list. A list of kitchen appliances she already owned. Stand mixer. Blender. Coffee maker. Air fryer. Instant Pot. She had two of some items. Her fiancé Mike had moved in six months ago, bringing his own duplicates.

"What do we put on the registry?" she asked. "We already have everything."

"Cash," I said. "Ask for cash."

She looked at me like I had suggested asking for lottery tickets. Then she thought about it. Then she smiled.

Jenna and Mike used their registry to cover half their wedding costs. They set up a cash fund instead of a traditional gift registry. Guests contributed $7,000. They put that money directly toward their venue and catering. No china. No crystal. No bread maker that would collect dust in the garage.

I asked Jenna if anyone complained. She said "my aunt asked why we did not have a traditional registry. I told her we already have everything we need. The only thing we need is to not start our marriage in debt." Her aunt ended up contributing $200 to the cash fund. Then she sent a separate check as a wedding gift.

Old rules? They do not apply anymore. According to industry data, more than 60% of couples now include cash funds on their registries. Some platforms — like Zola, The Knot, and Joy — make it easy. You can create specific funds: "Honeymoon flight," "Down payment on a house," "Couples cooking class," or even "Help us pay for our wedding."

Some couples worry that cash funds are tacky. They are not. They are practical. The older generation might not understand at first. Explain it gently. "We have lived together for two years. We already own two toasters. What we really need is help with the wedding so we can start our marriage debt-free."

I have seen this work beautifully. One couple set up a "honeymoon flight" fund. Guests could buy them a one-way ticket — $100 for the outbound, $100 for the return. They sold out within a week. Another couple set up a "date night" fund for their first year of marriage. Guests loved the idea of contributing to specific experiences.

You can create a separate line item for "expected registry contributions." As guests give, update the tracker. Seeing the number go up feels good. It is like a little cheer every time you log in.

I am not saying you should skip traditional gifts entirely. Some guests prefer to pick out a physical item. That is fine. Set up a hybrid registry. Ten physical items — nice sheets, a cast iron skillet, a coffee maker — plus a cash fund for the rest. Everyone wins.

Rachel had a brilliant hack. She put a few expensive physical items on her registry — a $600 stand mixer, a $400 espresso machine. She and her fiancé did not actually need those items. They knew their wealthy aunt would buy one of them. When the aunt did, Rachel returned it to the store for store credit. Then she used the credit to buy everyday household things. Same value, but more useful.

That is a little cheeky. But it is legal.

Take the estimated costs from the planner and turn them into registry items. "Flight to Mexico — $600." "Three nights at beachfront hotel — $900." "Sunset sailing excursion — $200." Guests feel like they are buying an experience, not just giving cash. The emotional connection is stronger.

Here is my step-by-step for setting up a registry that pays for your wedding.

Decide on your platform. Zola, The Knot, and Joy all have cash fund options. Compare fees. Some platforms charge a small percentage (2-3%) for credit card processing. Others are free if you use ACH transfer. Read the fine print.

Set up your funds. Be specific. "Help us pay for our venue" is fine. "Help us buy a house" is better. "First month of marriage date nights" is best. Specificity encourages giving.

Add a few physical items for older guests. A set of wine glasses. A cutting board. A nice picture frame. Keep the price range varied — $25, $50, $100, $200.

Share your registry link on your wedding website, on your invitations (discretely), and through word of mouth. Do not be pushy. Just make it easy to find.

Send thank-you notes immediately after the wedding. For cash funds, write a personal note: "Thank you for helping us start our marriage debt-free. Your gift made our wedding possible." That is not tacky. That is gratitude.

A friend of mine — not a client — told me she felt guilty about asking for cash. I asked her why. She said "it feels like I am asking my guests to pay for my wedding." I asked "did you ask them to come to your wedding?" Yes. "Do you expect them to bring a gift?" Yes. "What is the difference between a $100 physical gift and $100 cash?" She thought about it. "Nothing. The cash is more useful."

Exactly.

Cash is impersonal? The wedding industrial complex convinced us of that. It is not. It is honest. It is flexible. It helps you pay for the things you actually need.

Jenna and Mike — the couple who covered half their wedding costs through their registry — they are now saving for a house. They told me "every time we look at our savings account, we think of the family who helped us get here." That is not impersonal. That is love.
