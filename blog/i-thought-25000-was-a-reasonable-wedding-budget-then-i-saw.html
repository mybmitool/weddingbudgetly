I Thought 25000 Was A Reasonable Wedding Budget Then I Saw

2026-06-24 · 4 min read · General
General

I Thought $25,000 Was a Reasonable Wedding Budget. Then I Saw the Catering Quote.

I thought $25,000 was a reasonable wedding budget. I really did. I'm 29, I work in marketing in Portland, my fiancé is a software engineer, and between us we make $140,000 a year. We have savings. We have discipline. We have a spreadsheet. And I genuinely believed that $25,000 would be enough for a nice wedding in the Pacific Northwest. Not extravagant. Not destination. Just nice. A venue, some flowers, good food, a band, and 120 people who love us.

Then I got the catering quote. $18,500. For 120 people. That's $154 per person. For dinner. Not the venue. Not the flowers. Not the band. Just dinner. And it wasn't even lobster. It was chicken. Organic, free-range, locally sourced chicken with a side of seasonal vegetables and a starch. But still chicken.

I sat in my apartment in the Pearl District and stared at the number. My spreadsheet had allocated $8,000 for catering. I had based that on a Google search that said "average wedding catering cost is $70 per person." But that average was national. It included backyard weddings in Ohio and courthouse ceremonies in Kansas. It did not include Portland in 2026, where the wedding industry has inflated beyond recognition and every vendor acts like they're doing you a favor by existing.

I called my friend who got married last year. She spent $32,000. She had 100 people. Her catering was $12,000. Her venue was $8,000. Her flowers were $3,000. Her photographer was $4,000. Her dress was $2,500. And she told me she cut corners everywhere. She DIY'd the invitations. She bought the cake from a grocery store. She skipped the videographer. She used Spotify instead of a band. And she still spent $32,000.

I felt sick. Not because I couldn't afford it. Because I didn't want to afford it. I didn't want to spend $32,000 on a party. I didn't want to spend $25,000 on a party. I wanted to spend $15,000 and have it be beautiful and meaningful and not require me to eat ramen for two years.

So I did something radical. I redefined "wedding." Not the day. The budget. I asked myself: what do I actually care about? What will I remember in 10 years? What will my guests actually notice? And I cut everything else.

I cut the band. Spotify playlist. Saved $3,500. I cut the videographer. My cousin has a nice camera. Saved $2,500. I cut the flowers. I bought wholesale greenery from the Portland Flower Market and arranged it myself with my bridesmaids. Saved $2,000. I cut the fancy invitations. I used a digital invite with a beautiful design. Saved $800. I cut the wedding cake. I ordered 12 individual cakes from a local bakery and displayed them on a tiered stand. Saved $1,200. I cut the champagne toast. We served local wine and craft beer. Saved $1,500.

Total saved: $11,500. My new budget: $18,500. And I still had the venue I loved — a barn in the Willamette Valley with string lights and a view of the vineyards. I still had the photographer I wanted. I still had the dress. I still had the chicken. But I didn't have the things that didn't matter to me. The things that were for show. The things that guests would forget by Monday.

Here's what I learned. The wedding industry is designed to make you feel like you need everything. The favors. The signage. The specialty cocktails. The late-night snack. The photo booth. The sparkler exit. And if you want those things, great. But if you don't, you don't have to have them. You don't owe anyone a Pinterest wedding. You owe yourself a day that feels like you.

My wedding is in September. The budget is $18,500. The guest list is 120. The venue is booked. The photographer is booked. The dress is bought. And I'm not stressed about money. Because I made choices. Because I said no to things that didn't matter. Because I realized that the best wedding is not the most expensive one. It's the one where you can actually enjoy yourself without worrying about the bill.

If you're planning a wedding, do this. Make a list of what you care about. Make a list of what you don't. Cut the second list. Cut it aggressively. And then build a budget around the first list. Not around what the industry says you need. Around what you actually want.

— Olivia, from a barn in the Willamette Valley where the string lights are real and the budget is finally under control
